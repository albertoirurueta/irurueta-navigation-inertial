= Calibration algorithms
:description: The mathematics behind the calibrators: the linear least-squares system, the non-linear gravity-norm / field-norm formulation solved with Levenberg-Marquardt, robust estimation (RANSAC, MSAC, LMedS, PROSAC, PROMedS) and the Tedaldi et al. static-interval method.
:keywords: IMU calibration algorithms, linear least squares, normal equations, design matrix, Levenberg-Marquardt, gravity norm, RANSAC, MSAC, LMedS, PROSAC, PROMedS, Tedaldi, static interval, multi-position calibration

NOTE: This documentation was generated with the assistance of AI. Please report any inaccuracies.

The per-sensor pages (xref:calibration/accelerometer.adoc[], xref:calibration/gyroscope.adoc[],
xref:calibration/magnetometer.adoc[]) list *which* calibrator class to use. This page explains *how* the
calibrators work: the system of equations they build, how it is solved, how outliers are rejected and how
the turntable-free method of Tedaldi et al. (xref:reference.adoc#imu-tk[[imu-tk\]]) is put together from the
interval detectors, the measurement generators and the "Easy" calibrators.

All the derivations below start from the shared error model of xref:calibration/index.adoc[]. For the
accelerometer it reads

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas}} = \mathbf{b} + \left(\mathbf{I}_{3} + \mathbf{M}\right)\mathbf{f}_{\text{true}} + \mathbf{w}
++++

and the magnetometer uses the same form with stem:[\mathbf{b}_{\text{true}}] in place of
stem:[\mathbf{f}_{\text{true}}]. Throughout this page stem:[\mathbf{b}] is the bias (hard iron for the
magnetometer) and stem:[\mathbf{M}] is the matrix of scale-factor and cross-coupling errors, so
stem:[\mathbf{I}_3 + \mathbf{M}] is the full "gain" matrix of the sensor.

[#linear-model]
== Linear least squares (known true signal)

=== The error model is linear in its parameters

When the true signal stem:[\mathbf{f}_{\text{true}}] of every measurement is known (the `KnownFrame` and
`KnownBiasAndFrame` families, for every sensor), the unknowns stem:[\mathbf{b}] and stem:[\mathbf{M}] enter
the model linearly. Writing the entries of stem:[\mathbf{M}] as stem:[m_{ij}] (row stem:[i], column
stem:[j]; the diagonal entries are the scale factors stem:[s_x, s_y, s_z]) and moving the known term to the
left gives, per measurement stem:[k],

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas},k} - \mathbf{f}_{\text{true},k} = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{M}\,\mathbf{f}_{\text{true},k}
++++

Stack the three entries of stem:[\mathbf{b}] and the nine of stem:[\mathbf{M}] (column by column) into one
parameter vector

[stem]
++++
\boldsymbol{\theta} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{b} \\ \operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{M}) \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{12}
++++

Using the identity stem:[\mathbf{M}\mathbf{f} = (\mathbf{f}^{T} \otimes \mathbf{I}_3)\operatorname{vec}(\mathbf{M})],
every measurement contributes one *3-row block* to the system:

[stem]
++++
\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{I}_3 & \mathbf{f}_{\text{true},k}^{T} \otimes \mathbf{I}_3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{A}_k\ (3 \times 12)}
\boldsymbol{\theta}
= \underbrace{\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas},k} - \mathbf{f}_{\text{true},k}}_{\mathbf{y}_k\ (3 \times 1)}
++++

With stem:[N] measurements the blocks are stacked into the *design matrix* stem:[\mathbf{A}] (stem:[3N \times 12])
and the observation vector stem:[\mathbf{y}] (stem:[3N]):

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{A}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{A}_N \end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{y} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{y}_1 \\ \vdots \\ \mathbf{y}_N \end{bmatrix}
++++

The library orders the unknowns with the diagonal first, as
stem:[(b_x, b_y, b_z, s_x, s_y, s_z, m_{xy}, m_{xz}, m_{yx}, m_{yz}, m_{zx}, m_{zy})]. That is only a column
permutation of the vector above. In that ordering, and writing stem:[(f_x, f_y, f_z)] for the components of
stem:[\mathbf{f}_{\text{true},k}], the block of measurement stem:[k] is

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{A}_k =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 & f_x & 0 & 0 & f_y & f_z & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & f_y & 0 & 0 & 0 & f_x & f_z & 0 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & f_z & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & f_x & f_y
\end{bmatrix}
++++

The first row reads stem:[f_{\text{meas},x} - f_x = b_x + s_x f_x + m_{xy} f_y + m_{xz} f_z], which is just the
first component of the matrix equation above.

=== Normal equations and the least-squares solution

The system is over-determined as soon as there are more rows than unknowns, so the noise stem:[\mathbf{w}]
prevents an exact solution. The calibrator looks for the stem:[\boldsymbol{\theta}] that minimizes the sum of
squared residuals:

[stem]
++++
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \lVert \mathbf{A}\boldsymbol{\theta} - \mathbf{y} \rVert^{2}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A}\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{y}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \left(\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{y}
++++

`KnownFrameAccelerometerLinearLeastSquaresCalibrator` (and the equivalent gyroscope and magnetometer classes)
builds stem:[\mathbf{A}] and stem:[\mathbf{y}] exactly as above and hands them to the least-squares solve of
the algebra library, which returns the same minimizer as the normal equations without the need to form
stem:[\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A}] explicitly (squaring the matrix would square its condition number). The
estimated stem:[\hat{\mathbf{b}}] and stem:[\hat{\mathbf{M}}] are then copied out of
stem:[\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]. Because the problem is linear no initial guess is needed and the solution
is obtained in a single step.

For the `KnownBias...` variants the bias is moved to the left-hand side too
(stem:[\mathbf{y}_k = \mathbf{f}_{\text{meas},k} - \mathbf{f}_{\text{true},k} - \mathbf{b}]) and the first three
columns of stem:[\mathbf{A}_k] disappear, leaving only the entries of stem:[\mathbf{M}].

=== The common z-axis reduction

A general stem:[\mathbf{M}] has nine entries. The "common z-axis" assumption (`commonAxisUsed = true`)
declares that the z axis of the sensor frame coincides with the z axis of the orthogonal body frame and that
the x-y plane contains the sensor's y axis. This forces stem:[\mathbf{M}] to be *upper triangular*:

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{M} = \begin{bmatrix} s_x & m_{xy} & m_{xz} \\ 0 & s_y & m_{yz} \\ 0 & 0 & s_z \end{bmatrix}
++++

so stem:[m_{yx} = m_{zx} = m_{zy} = 0] and only six entries remain. The parameter vector drops from twelve
to *nine* unknowns (three biases plus six entries of stem:[\mathbf{M}]), the last three columns of every
stem:[\mathbf{A}_k] are removed, and the block becomes

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{A}_k =
\begin{bmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 & f_x & 0 & 0 & f_y & f_z & 0 \\
0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & f_y & 0 & 0 & 0 & f_z \\
0 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 & f_z & 0 & 0 & 0
\end{bmatrix}
++++

with unknowns stem:[(b_x, b_y, b_z, s_x, s_y, s_z, m_{xy}, m_{xz}, m_{yz})]. This is the same triangular
structure Tedaldi et al. impose on their accelerometer misalignment matrix (see <<tedaldi>>). In the
`Known...Frame...LinearLeastSquares` classes the flag defaults to `false`, i.e. the general twelve-unknown
case.

Each measurement contributes three scalar equations, so the linear solvers need at least stem:[\lceil n/3 \rceil]
measurements for stem:[n] unknowns. The classes enforce the following minimums (`MINIMUM_MEASUREMENTS`):

z-axis flag the system is smaller, so the same measurements give more redundancy.

NOTE: The gyroscope classes extend the model with the g-dependent term: the stacked equations become
stem:[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\text{meas}} - \boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\text{true}} = \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{M}_g\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\text{true}} + \mathbf{G}_g\mathbf{f}_{\text{true}}],
so each block has the columns of stem:[\mathbf{M}_g] multiplying stem:[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\text{true}}] followed by the nine
columns of stem:[\mathbf{G}_g] multiplying stem:[\mathbf{f}_{\text{true}}], for 21 unknowns in the general case.

=== Conditioning

The matrix stem:[\mathbf{A}^{T}\mathbf{A}] is only invertible, and well conditioned, if the true signals are
*diverse*. If every measurement is taken with the same stem:[\mathbf{f}_{\text{true}}] (for instance the sensor
always lying flat), the columns multiplying stem:[f_x], stem:[f_y] and stem:[f_z] are constant multiples of the
bias columns and the bias becomes indistinguishable from the scale factors. Good data therefore needs the
sensor held static in many different attitudes, covering all three axes in both directions. Measurement noise
is averaged out only if the number of measurements exceeds the minimum, so use more than the bare minimum
whenever possible.

[#nonlinear-model]
== Non-linear least squares (known norm only)

=== Why the problem becomes non-linear

The `KnownGravityNorm`, `KnownPosition`, `KnownMagneticFluxDensityNorm` and `KnownPositionAndInstant`
families do not know the *direction* of the true signal, only its *magnitude*: the device is held static in
unknown attitudes, so the accelerometer must read a vector of length stem:[\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert], and the
magnetometer a vector of the local field magnitude. The linear system above cannot be written (the right-hand
side is unknown) and the problem is solved with an iterative non-linear fit instead.

=== Reparametrization used by the solver

Inverting the model (with stem:[\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}} = \mathbf{I}_3 + \mathbf{M}]) gives the true signal as a
function of the measurement, and the solver internally optimizes the pair
stem:[(\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}, \mathbf{b}')], where stem:[\mathbf{b}'] is the bias expressed *before* the gain
matrix:

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas}} = \mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}\left(\mathbf{f}_{\text{true}} + \mathbf{b}'\right)
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\mathbf{f}_{\text{true}} = \mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}^{-1}\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas}} - \mathbf{b}',
\qquad \mathbf{b} = \mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}\mathbf{b}'
++++

The code comments state that this form converges better than optimizing stem:[\mathbf{b}] directly. After the
fit the result is converted back: stem:[\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}\mathbf{b}'] and
stem:[\mathbf{M} = \mathbf{M}_{\text{full}} - \mathbf{I}_3]. A user-supplied initial bias stem:[\mathbf{b}_0]
and matrix stem:[\mathbf{M}_0] are converted the same way: stem:[\mathbf{b}'_0 = (\mathbf{I}_3 + \mathbf{M}_0)^{-1}\mathbf{b}_0].

=== Cost function and residual

Let stem:[g] be the known magnitude (the gravity norm, or the field magnitude from the WMM; for
`KnownPosition` it is computed from the position with the gravity estimator and for
`KnownPositionAndInstant` from the world magnetic model). The model prediction for measurement stem:[k] is the
squared norm of the corrected measurement, and the observation is the squared known magnitude:

[stem]
++++
h_k(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \left\lVert \mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}^{-1}\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas},k} - \mathbf{b}' \right\rVert^{2},
\qquad y_k = g^{2}
++++

The Levenberg-Marquardt fitter minimizes the weighted chi-square

[stem]
++++
\chi^{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(\frac{y_k - h_k(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\sigma_k}\right)^{2}
++++

where stem:[\sigma_k] is the standard deviation attached to the measurement (the
`StandardDeviationBodyKinematics` specific-force deviation, or its magnetic counterpart). Working with squared
norms avoids a square root and keeps the cost smooth around zero. With
stem:[\mathbf{Q} = (\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}^{T})^{-1}] the prediction is
stem:[h_k = (\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas},k} - \mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}\mathbf{b}')^{T}\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas},k} - \mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}\mathbf{b}')],
i.e. the measurements are fitted with an *ellipsoid* (stem:[\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas}}] lies on the ellipsoid
centered at the bias with shape stem:[\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}^{T}]) that must map onto a sphere of radius
stem:[g] once corrected. See xref:calibration/magnetometer.adoc[] for the same picture applied to hard and soft iron.

[NOTE]
====
The prediction depends on stem:[\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}] only through
stem:[\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}^{T}], which has six degrees of freedom, so replacing
stem:[\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}] by stem:[\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}\mathbf{R}] (and stem:[\mathbf{b}'] by
stem:[\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{b}']) for any rotation stem:[\mathbf{R}] leaves the cost unchanged. The norm
constraint alone cannot fix the orientation of the sensor frame. Restricting stem:[\mathbf{M}] to the upper
triangular form of the common z-axis case removes exactly that freedom, which is why the common-axis problem has
nine unknowns, a unique minimum and is the better conditioned choice. In the general (twelve-unknown) case the
solver converges to one of the equivalent solutions, selected by the initial guess. For this reason the common
z-axis form is now the default of the norm-based accelerometer and magnetometer calibrators, and the general mode is
only meaningful up to a rotation of the sensor frame.

The weights stem:[\sigma_k] must be expressed in the units of the fitted quantity. The norm-based calibrators fit the
squared norm, so with a noise standard deviation stem:[\sigma_f] on the measurement they use
stem:[\sigma_k = 2\,g\,\sigma_f] (stem:[2\lVert\mathbf{B}\rVert\sigma_B] for the magnetometer), the first-order
propagation of stem:[\sigma_f] through stem:[\lVert\mathbf{f}\rVert^{2}].
====

=== Jacobian, damping and iteration

Levenberg-Marquardt linearizes the model around the current estimate, using the Jacobian
stem:[\mathbf{J}_{ki} = \partial h_k/\partial\theta_i], and solves a damped version of the Gauss-Newton step:

[stem]
++++
\left(\mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{J} + \lambda\,\operatorname{diag}\!\left(\mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{J}\right)\right)\delta\boldsymbol{\theta} = \mathbf{J}^{T}\mathbf{W}\,(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{h}),
\qquad \mathbf{W} = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\sigma_k^{-2}\right)
++++

If the step lowers stem:[\chi^2] it is accepted and the damping stem:[\lambda] is decreased (the iteration
behaves like Gauss-Newton); otherwise it is rejected and stem:[\lambda] is increased (the step shrinks toward
gradient descent). The iteration stops when stem:[\chi^2] no longer changes appreciably.

In this library the fitter is `LevenbergMarquardtMultiDimensionFitter` of `irurueta-numerical-computing` for the
norm-based calibrators, whose Jacobian row of each measurement is not coded analytically: the model
is wrapped in a `GradientEstimator` that obtains the partial derivatives numerically. The "Easy" gyroscope calibrators
have a three-component residual per sequence and use `LevenbergMarquardtMultiVariateFitter` with a numerical
Jacobian from a `JacobianEstimator` configured with a minimum step (see below). The `KnownFrame` non-linear
calibrators are the exception: their model is the linear one above, evaluated by a multivariate fitter that is
given the analytical Jacobian (the entries of stem:[\mathbf{A}_k]), and therefore converges almost immediately.

After convergence the fitter exposes the covariance matrix of the estimate, stem:[\chi^2], its degrees of
freedom, the reduced stem:[\chi^2], the mean squared error and the goodness-of-fit probabilities; the
calibrators publish these as `getEstimatedCovariance()`, `getEstimatedChiSq()`, `getEstimatedReducedChiSq()`,
`getEstimatedMse()`, `getEstimatedP()` and `getEstimatedQ()`. The covariance of stem:[(\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}, \mathbf{b}')]
is propagated to stem:[(\mathbf{b}, \mathbf{M})] through the Jacobian of the conversion above.

=== Initial guess and convergence

LM only finds a *local* minimum, so the starting point matters. The calibrators take it from the caller
(`setInitialBias`, `setInitialMa`, ... or the constructor arguments); if nothing is given the starting point is
stem:[\mathbf{b}_0 = \mathbf{0}] and stem:[\mathbf{M}_0 = \mathbf{0}] (an identity gain), which is a sensible default because real
sensors are close to ideal. A better start is any approximate estimate, for example a previous
calibration, the datasheet values, or the linear solution when a `KnownFrame` data set is available for the
same sensor. When the common z-axis flag is set, the lower triangle of the initial gain matrix is forced to zero
before inverting it. The solver then needs at least *one more measurement than unknowns* (the minimum is
stem:[n + 1]), which gives:

[#robust-estimation]
== Robust estimation

Every non-trivial calibrator has five `Robust...` variants (see xref:calibration/index.adoc[] for the family
tree). They all follow the same pattern. A *hypothesis* is computed by running the plain calibrator on a small random
subset of the measurements; the hypothesis is *scored* by how well it explains every measurement; the best
hypothesis wins; optionally the winner is *refined* using all the measurements it classifies as inliers.

=== Residuals

The residual of measurement stem:[k] for a hypothesis (stem:[\mathbf{b}], stem:[\mathbf{M}]) is the squared
disagreement with the known magnitude, using the user-facing parameters:

[stem]
++++
r_k = \left(g - \left\lVert (\mathbf{I}_3 + \mathbf{M})^{-1}\left(\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas},k} - \mathbf{b}\right)\right\rVert\right)^{2}
++++

For the `KnownFrame` families the residual instead compares the measurement predicted by the hypothesis from the
known true signal, stem:[\mathbf{b} + (\mathbf{I}_3 + \mathbf{M})\mathbf{f}_{\text{true},k}], with the actual one. Residuals are therefore in squared units of the signal (for example stem:[(\text{m/s}^2)^2]), which is
the unit of the threshold parameters below. If the hypothesis matrix cannot be inverted the residual is set to
the largest representable value.

=== Methods

[cols="1,3,2",options="header"]
|===
|Method |Sample selection and scoring |Main parameters

|RANSAC
|Random subsets. A measurement is an *inlier* when its residual is below `threshold`. The hypothesis with
the most inliers wins. The number of iterations adapts to the requested confidence and the inlier ratio seen so far.
|`threshold` (default `1e-2`), `confidence` (`0.99`), `maxIterations` (`5000`)

|MSAC
|Same random subsets, but the score is a *truncated cost*: every residual is clipped at `threshold` and the
clipped residuals are summed (smaller is better). Inliers contribute their actual error instead of a flat vote,
so among hypotheses with the same inlier count the tighter one wins.
|Same as RANSAC

|LMedS
|Random subsets, scored by the *median* of all residuals (smaller is better). No inlier threshold is needed
because the median is robust up to about half of outliers. The best median also yields the threshold used to classify inliers afterwards.
|`stopThreshold` (`1e-4`): iteration stops early when the best median falls below it; `confidence`, `maxIterations`

|PROSAC
|Like RANSAC, but measurements are *ordered by a quality score* (larger is better) and subsets are drawn
first from the best-ranked measurements, growing the pool progressively. Good measurements are found much
sooner when scores are informative.
|`threshold`, plus one quality score per measurement (`QualityScoredAccelerometerCalibrator` and the equivalents)

|PROMedS
|LMedS scoring with the PROSAC progressive sampling.
|`stopThreshold` plus quality scores
|===

The quality score of a measurement is any number you can attach to it (for example the inverse of its standard
deviation, or the length of the static interval it came from); only the ordering matters.

=== Practical notes

* The subset size defaults to the minimum number of measurements the plain calibrator needs and can be raised
  (`setPreliminarySubsetSize`), which makes each hypothesis less noisy at the cost of a lower chance of a clean sample.
* The refinement step (`refineResult`, `true` by default) runs the plain calibrator on the inliers only, starting
  from the best hypothesis, and falls back to the unrefined hypothesis if it fails. The covariance is kept by default (`keepCovariance`).
* The inlier flags and per-measurement residuals can be retained (`computeAndKeepInliers`, `computeAndKeepResiduals`) and are
  always available when refinement is on.
* RANSAC and MSAC are sensitive to the `threshold`; LMedS and PROMedS need none, which is convenient when the
  noise level is not known beforehand.

[#tedaldi]
== The Tedaldi et al. method

Tedaldi, Pretto and Menegatti (xref:reference.adoc#imu-tk[[imu-tk\]]) describe a procedure that needs no
turntable or external reference: the operator holds the IMU still for an initial period, then moves it between
a set of distinct *static* attitudes. The accelerometer triad is calibrated from the static attitudes alone, and
the gyroscope triad is then calibrated by checking that integrating the angular rate between two static
attitudes reproduces the change of the gravity direction measured by the already calibrated accelerometer.
The library splits this into detection, measurement generation and calibration stages.

[mermaid]
....

    D -->|"static / dynamic status"| G["MeasurementsGenerator<br/>(AccelerometerMeasurementsGenerator,<br/>GyroscopeMeasurementsGenerator)"]
    G -->|"one StandardDeviationBodyKinematics<br/>per static interval"| A["Accelerometer calibrators<br/>KnownGravityNorm, KnownPosition"]
    G -->|"one BodyKinematicsSequence<br/>per dynamic interval"| E["EasyGyroscopeCalibrator<br/>KnownBiasEasyGyroscopeCalibrator"]
    A -->|"b and M of the accelerometer"| E
    E --> R["Gyroscope b, M and G"]
....

=== Static-interval detection

Detection works on the accelerometer stream. After an initial period during which the device must not move
(`initialStaticSamples`, default 5000 samples), the detector measures the base noise level: the norm of the per-axis
variances accumulated over that period. For every new sample it computes the same variance norm over a sliding window
(`windowSize`, default 101 samples) and applies a simple test:

[stem]
++++
v_{w}(t) = \sqrt{\operatorname{var}_{x}^{2}(t) + \operatorname{var}_{y}^{2}(t) + \operatorname{var}_{z}^{2}(t)},
\qquad
\text{status} =
\begin{cases}
\text{static} & v_{w}(t) < \kappa\,v_{0} \\
\text{dynamic} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
++++

where stem:[v_0] is the base noise level and stem:[\kappa] is `thresholdFactor` (default 4.0). This is the detector of the
paper/imu_tk, which compares the magnitude of the windowed variances with a multiple of the one measured in the initial
period. Earlier versions of the library used the norm of the standard deviations (default factor 2.0); because the
statistic is now based on variances, a factor stem:[k] on standard deviations corresponds to stem:[k_{\text{reference}}
\approx k^{2}] here, and every previously tuned factor changes meaning. As in imu_tk the window is *centred*: the
status is assigned to the sample at the centre of the window, so every status change is delayed half a window
(`getDetectionDelaySamples()`), in exchange for symmetric boundaries and static means free of motion samples. The paper
estimates the multiplier automatically inside its calibration loop; the library instead exposes it as a parameter, and the
generator documentation recommends repeating the calibration with several threshold factors, because the gyroscope
calibration is very sensitive to where intervals start and end.
Two safeguards act during initialization: if the windowed variance norm suddenly exceeds the accumulated one by
`instantaneousNoiseLevelFactor` (default 9.0, it was 2.0), or the base level is above `baseNoiseLevelAbsoluteThreshold`
(now a variance), the detector fails and must be reset, because the initial period was not still.

=== Measurements for the accelerometer

While the detector reports a static interval, the accumulated average of the accelerometer samples is the
single input measurement of that interval:

[stem]
++++
\bar{\mathbf{f}}_{\text{meas}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{f}_{\text{meas},j}
++++

When the interval ends (a transition to dynamic), `AccelerometerMeasurementsGenerator` emits one
`StandardDeviationBodyKinematics` whose specific force is that mean and whose standard deviation is the average
of the three per-axis deviations divided by stem:[\sqrt{n}] (the standard deviation of a mean of stem:[n] samples).
The initialization period is static, so its samples belong to the first static interval. Intervals shorter than `minStaticSamples` (default two windows) are discarded as unreliable,
and a dynamic interval longer than `maxDynamicSamples` (default 30 windows) is skipped. Averaging suppresses
the noise, so the noise term can be dropped from the model, as the paper does.

=== Accelerometer cost (multi-position method)

In each static attitude the corrected measurement must have the magnitude of gravity. With the paper's model
stem:[\mathbf{a}^{O} = \mathbf{T}_a\mathbf{K}_a(\mathbf{a}^{S} + \mathbf{b}_a)] (upper triangular
misalignment stem:[\mathbf{T}_a], diagonal scaling stem:[\mathbf{K}_a], bias stem:[\mathbf{b}_a]) the cost over the
stem:[M] static intervals is

[stem]
++++
L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{acc}}) = \sum_{k=1}^{M}\left(\lVert\mathbf{g}\rVert^{2} - \left\lVert h(\mathbf{a}^{S}_k, \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{acc}})\right\rVert^{2}\right)^{2},
\qquad h(\mathbf{a}^{S},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{acc}}) = \mathbf{T}_a\mathbf{K}_a(\mathbf{a}^{S} + \mathbf{b}_a)
++++

with nine parameters (three misalignments, three scale factors, three biases), minimized with Levenberg-Marquardt.
This is exactly the cost of xref:calibration/algorithms.adoc#nonlinear-model[the norm-based formulation above]: stem:[\mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}^{-1}]
plays the role of stem:[\mathbf{T}_a\mathbf{K}_a] and the paper's bias, expressed on the measured side, is the
negative of the library's stem:[\mathbf{b}] (stem:[\mathbf{b}_a = -\mathbf{b}]), and the upper-triangular common z-axis case has the same nine unknowns. In the library it is
implemented by `KnownGravityNormAccelerometerCalibrator` (gravity norm given) or `KnownPositionAccelerometerCalibrator`
(gravity norm computed from the position), fed with the generator's measurements.

=== Gyroscope cost (integrated rotation vs. gravity direction)

The gyroscope calibration uses the *dynamic* intervals between consecutive static intervals. Let
stem:[\mathbf{u}_{a,k}] be the unit gravity direction measured by the *calibrated* accelerometer averaged in the
stem:[k]-th static interval, and let stem:[\Psi] be an attitude integrator that, given the angular-rate samples of the motion between
intervals stem:[k-1] and stem:[k] and the starting direction stem:[\mathbf{u}_{a,k-1}], returns the predicted
direction stem:[\mathbf{u}_{g,k}]:

[stem]
++++
\mathbf{u}_{g,k} = \Psi\!\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}_i^{S},\ \mathbf{u}_{a,k-1}\right),
\qquad
L(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{gyro}}) = \sum_{k=2}^{M}\left\lVert \mathbf{u}_{a,k} - \mathbf{u}_{g,k}\right\rVert^{2}
++++

The integration advances a quaternion with the kinematics equation stem:[\dot{\mathbf{q}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Omega}(\boldsymbol{\omega})\mathbf{q}]
using a fourth-order Runge-Kutta step (the paper's RK4n, followed by a re-normalization of the quaternion).

`EasyGyroscopeCalibrator` implements this cost as follows. Each *sequence* (one `BodyKinematicsSequence`: the mean
specific force of the preceding static interval, the samples of the movement, and the mean specific force of the
following static interval) is one observation:

. The two static means are corrected with the accelerometer bias and cross-coupling errors supplied by the user
  (that is why the calibrator is an `AccelerometerDependentGyroscopeCalibrator`) and normalized to unit vectors, so the
  result does not depend on the local gravity magnitude. The pair of directions is stored as the input row, and the target stem:[y_k] is
  zero.
. For the current parameter guess, every sample of the sequence is corrected with the gyroscope model,
  stem:[\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\text{true}} = \mathbf{M}_{\text{full}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Omega}_{\text{meas}} - \mathbf{b}' - \mathbf{G}'\mathbf{f}_{\text{true}}]
  (the same reparametrization as before, with the g-dependent term when it is being estimated).
. The corrected angular rates are integrated with `QuaternionIntegrator` using the Runge-Kutta step to obtain the
  rotation stem:[\mathbf{q}] over the sequence.
. The starting gravity direction is rotated by the inverse of that attitude change (the body frame turns while gravity is
  fixed) and compared with the measured end direction; the residual is the Euclidean distance between the two
  unit vectors.

The residual is the three-component difference stem:[\mathbf{u}_{a,k} - \mathbf{R}_k^{T}\mathbf{u}_{a,k-1}] (rather than the
norm of that difference), weighted by the gravity-direction standard deviation of the sequence
(`BodyKinematicsSequence.getGravityDirectionStandardDeviation()`), and minimized with `LevenbergMarquardtMultiVariateFitter` over
stem:[\mathbf{b}], stem:[\mathbf{M}_g] (and optionally stem:[\mathbf{G}_g]), using a numerical Jacobian from a
`JacobianEstimator` with a relative and a minimum step of `1e-7`. Differences from the paper that were kept: the library takes the gyroscope bias as an
unknown of the fit (the paper averages the gyroscope in the initial still period and removes it), and it has an
optional upper-triangular common z-axis form. Following the paper, the defaults of `EasyGyroscopeCalibrator` are now the
general (full) matrix and *no* g-dependent term (`commonAxisUsed = false`, `estimateGDependentCrossBiases = false`);
the upper-triangular form is only valid for the sensor defining the body frame (the accelerometer), and the g-dependent matrix
is weakly observable from gravity references only. Each sequence provides three equations, but the calibrator keeps a
conservative minimum of one more sequence than unknowns:

[cols="3,1,1",options="header"]
|===
|Configuration |Unknowns |Minimum sequences

|Common z-axis, no g-dependent term
|9
|10

|General, no g-dependent term (default)
|12
|13

|Common z-axis, with g-dependent term
|18
|19

|General, with g-dependent term
|21
|22
|===

Each sequence must also contain a few body kinematics items (the Javadoc asks for at least three). Gyroscope
sequences are generated only when both neighbouring static intervals are valid, dynamic intervals around a too short
static interval are joined, and the sequence is emitted when the following static interval ends (see
xref:calibration/intervals-and-generators.adoc#generators[the generators]).

Deliberate deviations from the paper/imu_tk that were kept: the accelerometer cost is the squared norm residual
(with standard deviation stem:[2\,g\,\sigma]) instead of the norm, the Turntable calibrators keep their previous
defaults (common z-axis and g-dependent term), and the minimum number of sequences and measurements stays at the number
of unknowns plus one.

=== Putting it together

. Keep the device still for the initial period (long enough for `initialStaticSamples`).
. Move it to a new attitude and hold it still, many times, producing alternating static and dynamic intervals.
. Feed every sample through an `AccelerometerAndGyroscopeMeasurementsGenerator` (or the individual accelerometer and gyroscope
  generators); collect the generated `StandardDeviationBodyKinematics` and `BodyKinematicsSequence` instances.
. Calibrate the accelerometer with `KnownGravityNorm`/`KnownPosition` using the first collection.
. Pass the estimated accelerometer bias and `Ma` plus the sequences to `EasyGyroscopeCalibrator`
  (or `KnownBiasEasyGyroscopeCalibrator` when the gyroscope bias is known), optionally wrapped by a robust variant.

See xref:calibration/intervals-and-generators.adoc[] for the detector and generator classes in detail.
